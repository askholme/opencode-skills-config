# Objection Handling

Objections are information. Don't argue. Ask more questions about the objection, then address the real one.

## The universal first move

Ask what the deal breaker is. Don't accept the surface answer as an endpoint.

## Common objections and responses

**"My gut says no."**
Dig. Ask where the feeling comes from, it's rarely the real reason:
> "Where does that come from? What's really behind it?"

Almost always the real objection follows, one you can actually address.

**"It feels like a step backwards, we're redoing ICP and offer."**
Reframe immediately:
> "I'm not saying we throw everything out. Usually it's more: how are you packaging and marketing it? That's different from starting from scratch."

**"I've already invested a lot in coaches. Why would this be different?"**
Be honest. Don't make promises you can't keep:
> "I don't know who you've worked with. The clients I've been working with lately are all winning. I definitely don't work with everyone. If I don't get a good result with you, it doesn't sell itself further either. That matters more to me than a quick deal."

**"Will I learn enough to close my own deals?"**
Clarify what the coaching is:
> "I'm not going to close for you. That's exactly what the coaching is: DM scripts, preparing sales calls, offer language. You learn it. I help you do it well."

**"Six months is a lot. Can it be shorter?"**
Three months minimum. Explain why:
> "Three months is the minimum for momentum. Six months gives enough room to really embed it."

**"I don't know if I can make that payment every month."**
Offer an alternative payment structure, never a discount:
> "You can also pay upfront, there's something off for that. Or we split it into two instalments. But the amount itself doesn't change."

**"I want to work month-to-month."**
Don't offer it. Explain why:
> "It takes time to create and improve content and turn conversations with your ICP into business, so I don't offer a month-to-month option."

**"I don't have the time capacity."**
Don't lower the bar. Find where the work goes:
> Ask what could be delegated (assistant, team member). Clarify what needs their judgement vs. what's functional. Offer to train the delegate in the first sessions.

**"No budget."**
They often have it, but the lead doesn't trust you (yet). Try to discover why.

**"Timing isn't right."**
Ask what happens if they don't take action. Explain the outcome if they do act now. Refer to their initial reason for contacting you; this realigns them with their goals.

**"Not interested." / "Thanks, but no."**
Don't retreat with "thank you for your time", that's submissive and yields nothing. Get the reason:
> "Hey, totally understand. Just to know: what would make this a no-brainer for you?"

Their answer is either a reopened conversation or free offer research. Both beat silence.

**"That's above my budget right now" (after an early price reveal).**
Don't argue, don't discount. Park it warmly and reopen later:
> "No problem at all, I'll keep you in mind. Good luck!"

Then weeks or months later, reopen on pain and goals, not the offer:
> "Hey [name], I'm curious: what did you originally want to solve or achieve through [your service area]?"

The conversation died at price. Restart it at the problem.

## Other moves to turn it into a yes

- Offer a shorter entry if duration is the blocker: "What if we take three months as the starting point and see from there?"
- Share your screen again if they're unsure. A second look at the workspace often works better than more words
- Name a relevant client example that matches their exact objection
- Tell them you're selective: "I only take it on when I know I can win. If you don't get a good result, that doesn't help me either."
- If the doubt is about self-confidence: acknowledge it, connect it to a client where that was also a factor
